# The Best DnD 5e Puzzle Ideas

Puzzles can accomplish a variety of things. They can tell a story, get players to think about the nature of a *D&D* adventure, or simply be entertaining. Several puzzles have become popular throughout the annals of *D&D*, thanks to their classic nature and versatility. When used creatively, these puzzles will add another layer of depth to any dungeon.



## 35 Blow Your Player's Minds With a Mindscape
**These Mental Planes Can Be Anything Their Creator Can Imagine**

For players who have experienced a lot of Dungeons and Dragons, it can be hard to surprise them with something they haven't seen before. To turn players' expectations on their heads, sending them somewhere where the normal rules don't apply can shock and engage them out of their normal patterns of behavior. Mindscapes, sometimes referred to as dreamscapes, are a reality that exists completely within a creature's psyche, and thus are able to follow whatever rules the Dungeon Master wants to enforce.

With the rules of reality thrown out, anything can happen. Monsters can have new nightmarish abilities, gravity or time can be rendered meaningless, and magic can be altered or disabled. Death itself can even become optional, letting cruel DMs truly frighten players before an "it's all a dream" reveal. While this level of "not normal" can be a shot of adrenaline to bored players, it's important that they have an avenue out, whether it be waking the mind that has ensnared them or destroying the dreamscape in some way to escape.


## 34 Flooding Chambers Are a Timed Trap With a Twist
**These Tense Traps Can Provide an Iconic Encounter**

Dungeon traps are endearing in their over-the-top protective measures, and a flooding room might be among the most iconic dungeon traps around. After some trigger, the room gradually fills with water or some other substance until the trap is disabled, circumvented, or finished with its deadly business.

Variants on this trap can include using sand or another substance instead of water, or even adding monsters like piranhas or insect swarms to complicate the hunt for countermeasures. It might be possible to disable the trap, shunting the room's contents down a drain, or the players might need to find a way out of the room before it finishes suffocating them.

## 33 One-Way Passages Force Players To Explore More Recklessly
**If Players Won’t Split the Party Themselves, Sometimes They Need a Little Push**

A one-way passageway is a great way to push characters to make choices that push caution to the side. While low-level characters can be discombobulated with a simple one-way door or a pit-to-a-tunnel trap, high-level variations can include magic doorways or passageways too small for the whole party.

One-way passages either force your whole party to explore a section of the dungeon they can't retreat from, or send the party down different paths through the dungeon until they're able to find a way back to one another. When the adventuring party isn't at full strength, players are forced to try new tactics and out-of-the-box thinking against any encounters they trigger, and can make the dungeon feel more like a place of danger rather than just a series of fights to check off.

## 32 An Illusion of a Trap Provides the Danger Without the Damage
**This Comedic Trick Can Lighten the Mood of a Long Dungeon Crawl**

If there's anything funnier than watching low-level players struggle with a simple pit trap, it's watching low-level players struggle with a pit trap that isn't really there. Using illusion spells to make players think that there is a trap when there isn't creates a comical set-up that still challenges players' characters and creativity. If some members of the party realize that there isn't really a trap there, they can be rewarded with a funny description of their stricken party members falling to the floor, convinced that they just fell two stories while they're really just laying down.

Half the fun of this trap is the comical natural consequences. If a low-Will fighter can't disbelieve the illusory trap, then other party members might need to drop them a length of rope to help them "climb" out of the pit. Watching players struggle against a trap that isn't really hurting them makes for a funny change of pace for both players and game masters.


## 31 Give Your Trap a Speaking Role

A staple of the fantasy genre, intelligent items are a great way to make your dungeons a little more lively. Sentient objects make great additions to dungeons and can serve equally well as allies or malevolent entities. A sentient object might be part of the dungeon's greater mystery, like a demon bound to an object as a guardian spirit, or could be a non-player character in their own right, like an animated door giving players quests to accomplish or riddles to solve before opening the door.

Intelligent objects can even be treasure, though you should consider carefully whether an intelligent object has a greater role in your campaign before bringing one to your table. Intelligent objects have their own personalities and can disagree with their wielders, refusing to share their power with those they aren't aligned with. Part of the puzzle can be how to foster good relations with your new companion in order to unlock their full potential.

## 30 The Gentleman's Doorway Requires Proper Behavior
**Manners Prevail in This Charming Puzzle**

Sometimes, the most obscure ideas can make for the best *D&D* puzzles, since players probably won't recognize these puzzles/traps and their inner workings. These obscure original traps don't have to be complicated, such as the Gentleman's Door, since the mystery is the real challenge.

The Gentleman's Doorway creates a double of any player character who wishes to pass through into a desired room, and that double always mimics the PC's movements. The solution is for the player to yield to the gentleman double rather than force their way through, thus being the real gentleman. The double will reciprocate this kindness and step aside to let the player through first.


## 29 Painting Passwords Can Be Assembled for Solutions
**A Gallery Can Become a Puzzle for a Creative Gamemaster**

Passwords are the basis of many simple but fun *D&D* puzzles, such as solving riddles where the answer is the password. Another idea is to break up the password into pieces and challenge the party to find and assemble the components of a password or passphrase.

To get through a door that requires a password, the party must look around and realize that the numerous paintings on the walls hide the solution. On the back of each painting is either a letter or word as part of the solution, and once the pieces are assembled, a word or sentence is put together.

## 28 Chain Door Lifting Puzzles Are More Physical Feats
**A Puzzle Even a Barbarian Can Solve**

Some *D&D* puzzles call for some tactile work and not just solving puzzles like Sudoku or clever wordplay with a sphinx. Physically demanding puzzles can help add some variety and allow strong party members like a barbarian or a fighter to feel more useful outside of combat, too.

One example is a puzzle involving a heavy door that can only be raised if two chains are pulled hard enough simultaneously. The trick is that those chains are at least 30 feet apart and must be held so the door stays up — if they're released, the door quickly slams shut again. The party must also find a way to keep those chains in place so everyone can pass through the lifted door.

## 27 Solving a Cryptex Can Allow Players to Show Their Cleverness
**The One-Word Solution Can Be an Important Campaign Clue**

Fans of the author Dan Brown will recognize the concept of a cryptex. It's a tube-shaped device with rotating segments with letters or numbers on them, and the segments can be turned to form a word or number. The correct solution will unlock the tumblers inside and allow the cryptex to open.

A *D&D* party might receive the cryptex ahead of time, then search a room, dungeon, or an entire mission area to find possible solutions to the passcode on that device. The party will need whatever's inside the cryptex to continue their quest, such as a key or a rolled-up map. Ideally, the cryptex will resist being physically broken open somehow.

## 26 False Pillars/Supports Can Hide New Secrets
**Hidden Spiral Stairwells Can Reveal Whole New Floors of a Dungeon**

  The dungeon master can hide a secret passageway or item inside a false item in a dungeon, though searching every dungeon feature is tedious. The party may get a hint that the dungeon itself hides the desired item, something that's "wrong" or out of place.

If a party member is an expert dungeon crawler or a skilled architect/builder, they may eventually realize that a certain support beam or pillar is in the wrong place or shouldn't be there at all. The party might also obtain a blueprint or map of the dungeon to identify the wrong pillar when they find it.

## 25 Puzzle Cubes Can Be Modified to Challenge Players
**Characters Need To Find All the Pieces To Solve These Puzzles**

The Tomb of Annihilation module has plenty of huge dungeons and cool D&D puzzles, including the presence of puzzle cubes. In one sequence, the party must scour a city's temples for puzzle cubes depicting trickster gods, competing with the Red Wizards to find them.

A DM could lift that puzzle straight out of *Tomb of Annihilation*, or they could make a modified version to suit the story and themes of their campaigns. The carvings on those puzzle cubes, the proper solution, and even the number of cubes, can be modified to make the puzzle easier or tougher for *D&D* players to solve.

## 24 Infernal Puzzle Box Can Hide Treasures for Players
**Solving the Puzzle Is the Only Way Inside These Magic Boxes**

Infernal puzzle boxes are cool magical items that can safely store practically anything that fits inside them, such as a contract that a mortal signed with one of the *Dungeons & Dragons* universe's devils. Such puzzle boxes have unbreakable magical seals, and they can't be physically damaged, either.

The only option is to solve the puzzle on the outside of the box, which will require a pretty serious INT check from any player character who's determined to break into it. A clever D&D DM might hide anything in the puzzle box, from valuable treasure to a MacGuffin to a prank item no one expected.

## 23 A Riddle Sphinx Or Doorway Can Allow Creativity for Players and DMs
**Riddles Have Been a Plot Device Since Ancient Times**

Riddles in *Dungeons & Dragons* can easily become one component in a larger puzzle, but a DM can make the riddle the sole puzzle if they wish. To make this relatively simple puzzle more substantial, a DM can tie a riddle to a doorway or a sphinx that will only grant admittance once the riddle is solved.

The DM might set up several riddles, and the sphinx will demand the party answer each one correctly to pass. The DM could make each riddle relatively simple to make this a compelling multi-stage challenge. Perhaps the session's storyline or setting could provide much-needed clues.

## 22 An Empty-Handed Statue Can Confound Players
**Only by Giving Can the Players Receive**

Some players might suspect that every statue they find is an iconic *Dungeons & Dragons* monster like a Mimic in disguise, a hostile stone golem, or a gargoyle waiting to attack. Statues can also be mere decorations, but the most cunning *D&D* puzzles can turn a statue into something to solve.

An empty-handed statue in *D&D* undoubtedly wants an item put into its hand, but the players won't immediately know what the desired item is. Clues such as nearby monsters, journal entries, or an NPC's dialogue could provide the perfect hint. This is one of the most creative ways to engage players in puzzle-solving.

## 21 An Unexpected Clone Can Wreak Havoc in D&D Parties
**Who Doesn’t Love a Shapeshifter Causing Mischief?**

Experienced players know Doppelgänger are humanoid monsters that can perfectly mimic NPCs, but they might also mimic a party member. A DM who opts for this *D&D* puzzle can speak with players privately to set up the puzzle, having that player portray their real character and the clone.

This challenges the other party members of a *D&D* campaign to determine which is the real character, and which is a cunning fake. It can be especially fun for the Doppelgänger player, who may tease their teammates with two versions of their character before gradually giving away who's who.

## 20 The Pythagorean Cup or Greedy Cup Puzzles Are Classics for a Reason
**This Ancient Puzzle Remains Difficult Today**

The Pythagorean Cup is one of those "classic" *D&D* puzzles that can be traced back to ancient times. Also known as the Greedy Cup or the Cup of Justice, this drinking vessel has a small hole. If the water inside gets filled past a certain point, the cup's contents begin leaking out the bottom. The most common way to incorporate the Pythagorean Cup into a campaign is to turn the cup into a large pool of liquid and place it in a room.

Fill the Pythagorean pool with a dangerous liquid — like boiling water, lava, or acid — and commission the players to get something from the bottom of the pool. Players can turn a nob or crank to add more liquid. Once the Pythagorean pool is filled past a certain point, the liquid will be siphoned out, letting players access whatever was previously submerged.

## 19 Out-of-Place Monsters Add an Element of Surprise
**Their Unexpected Presence Creates Intriguing Backstory**

Monsters add flavor to every *D&D* combat encounter. Whether fighting goblins in a twisting network of caves or battling an ancient dragon in its lair of hoarded treasure, having the right monster in a setting heightens the *D&D* experience. The same can be said when the wrong monster appears in a given setting. If players are traversing the snowy winter tundra of the far north, let them encounter a powerful devil from the Abyss.

If they're traveling by boat down a roaring river, have a zombie tyrannosaurus rise from the waters to attack. Surprising players is always a valuable hook for keeping them engaged. Maybe the barbarian tribes of the tundra made a pact with a devil to survive, or the river changed course over centuries and now flows over an ancient lich’s tomb. An out-of-place monster is jarring for players, but if this encounter leads to them uncovering a greater mystery, it will become a rewarding experience.

## 18 Timed Puzzles Increase Tension for Players
**A Ticking Clock Heightens the Drama of Any Adventure**

Adding a time limit to any *D&D* task increases the tension. If players try to crack a code within a dungeon, the DM should add an hourglass. When solving a murder, DM's can task the players to uncover the culprit before the killer strikes again. No matter the type of countdown, there are three things every DM needs to consider.

First, figure out how much time to give the players — the task should be challenging, but not impossible. Second, there needs to be a reliable way for players to keep track of their time. Finally, the DM should present clear consequences for failure, adding more weight to the task at hand.

## 17 River Crossing Puzzles Have Their Roots in Antiquity
**It Takes Careful Planning To Make Sure Everything Survives the Journey Across**

The River Crossing Puzzle is an old logic game first proposed in the Dark Ages. The original premise is that a farmer must transport a bag of grain, a duck, and a fox across a river, but can only carry one at a time in a tiny boat. While transporting, the farmer can't leave the duck alone with the grain (which the duck would eat), or the fox with the duck (who the fox would eat).

A DM can modify these three items for a campaign in many ways. For example, a magic item, a thief, and a vengeful paladin wronged by the thief might all need to be brought across the river to testify in court. There are also variants of this logic puzzle known as the “Bridge and Torch Problem” and the “Missionaries and Cannibals Problem.” Both could make for great dungeon encounters or escort missions, especially if the DM replaces the cannibals with a classic monster for more brutal D&D puzzle scenarios.

## 16 Water Pouring Puzzles Are a Classic Conundrum
**This Puzzle Requires Careful Mathematical Measuring**

The most common version of a *Dungeons & Dragons* Water Pouring Puzzle features three jugs of different sizes and a finite amount of water or some similar liquid. To tackle this, the party of players must figure out how much water to pour into each container to solve the puzzle.

In some cases, *D&D* players must pass the water back and forth between the correct vessels to acquire the desired amount. When the puzzle is solved, it can trigger whatever magical effects the DM desires, be it opening a hidden door or ending a powerful curse. This is another engaging and meticulous puzzle that DMs can use to shake up a campaign.


## 15 The Count Down Can Be a Great Practical Joke When Timed Correctly
**Nothing Panics Players Like Believing Some Imminent Catastrophe Is About To Happen**

The Count Down is a hilarious and fun puzzle that's more of a practical joke than anything else. The *D&D* players stand in a room, furnished with thematic objects of the DM's choice to set the scene. Symbols are spread across the head of the door in front of them, usually between 10-30 symbols. In the center of the room, there is a pedestal with some form of switch.

When the *D&D* players activate the switch, the symbols on the door begin to glow one by one at a rate of roughly one per second. These symbols act like a countdown, giving the situation an ominous atmosphere. When the countdown ends, the door opens. The trick is to make the players believe something bad will happen. When evaluating the door, they'll likely discuss what to do and what the symbols mean. They may even return to town for supplies or rest. In the end, the players will be in for a shocking surprise.

## 14 Mirror Puzzles Can Reveal Clues to Players
**The Mirror Shows More Than Just a Reflection**

To pull off a Mirror puzzle, use the mirror to reflect the true state of a room. The inside of a room may look one way, but the mirror portrays it differently. Making things more interesting, the DM can leave objects scattered across the room that must be placed in their correct spots.

Clues and symbols hint at where the objects should be placed. However, the only way the players can see the clues is by peering into the mirror. Mirror puzzles are malleable and allow the DM to show off their creativity. Additionally, the DM can make them as simple or challenging as they wish.

## 13 Plates on the Floor Can Hide New Dangers
**Characters Need To Watch Where They Step**

Spicing things up to an extent, the DM can use an image or riddle to describe a path the adventurers must walk along a series of trigger plates. Should they fail, the person who steps on the wrong plate suffers a penalty, damage, a debuff, or whatever the DM decides.

Once the adventurers walk the correct path based on the riddle or puzzle, they clear the area. An image of a snake on the ceiling is a good way to hint at the path. Vindictive DMs can use a riddle like "The fastest way between two points" and watch the players stumble about in confusion, failing to walk in a straight line as the riddle suggests.

## 12 Weighing Your Problems Puzzles Are Simple, but Elegant
**Pound for Pound, This Puzzle Is a Great Value**

Puzzles involving weights in *D&D* are simple, elegant, and a great way to get players to a higher floor in the dungeon. To pull it off, a DM will need a kind of scale apparatus with a series of heavy objects like rubble or debris nearby.

The players must put the correct weight on one end of the scale so a party member can be lifted to the next level and drop down a rope for the rest. Such a puzzle isn't too difficult to solve, but it's certainly a lot of fun — especially if the players don't have a rope and must catapult the last member up using the device.

## 11 Judge and Jury Makes Players Determine the Culprit

The Judge and Jury puzzle can have a variety of settings, but the crux is that players must decide who is guilty of a crime. There are numerous scenarios to make this work. In a town, the basic setup involves a person who's been murdered and the suspects — the victim's violent parent who's prone to rage and a demure servant who seems honest and pitiful. If presented as a riddle in a dungeon, things become more interesting.

If the dungeon is aligned with some evil force, the correct answer is to proclaim the servant is guilty for the party to pass the test. In a Neutral aligned area, players must answer that there isn't enough information to convict either. In a place aligned with good, the parent must be accused. The penalty for the wrong answer is up to the DM.

## 10 The Truth Can Open Up New Possibilities in D&D Campaigns
**Found Items Can Reveal a Secret Backstory**

A Truth puzzle focuses on finding the correct pages in journals, putting them in the right order, and uncovering a truth of some sort. In one case, the *D&D* party may stumble across some soldiers who died in battle. In each soldier's possession are notes to scrutinize.

By finding and ordering the notes, the players will discover the truth about that battle, uncovering any conspiracies the DM chooses to concoct. A great way for the DM to shake things up even more is to incorporate a restless spirit haunting a place or person. Learning the truth will vindicate the restless spirit and allow the true villain to be punished.

## 9 That Unreadable Map Can Allow Creativity in Dungeon Crawling
**Does That Scribble Say “Pit” or “Put”?**

The *D&D* party will encounter a map in some cases that displays a dungeon in some detail, but most of the writing is illegible. Deciphering the legible text will give the party clues concerning where the traps are and where the treasure is in a particular dungeon. However, the party only has the benefit of the legible words and no more.

This will force the players to consider the context of the words being used, which can get very interesting when the words have multiple meanings. The DM can get especially creative if they add in other elements and conditions. For example, the map may need to be folded in a specific way to be read.

## 8 Show and Tell Can be a D&D Dad Joke
**This Logic Puzzle Has a Groan-Worthy Solution**

Show and Tell remains one of the funnier *D&D* puzzles and the DM equivalent of a dad joke. Its premise involves the party discovering a room with a large face carved from stone. The face is staring at a sentence written in some place prominent in the puzzle room. The sentence reads "Show Me Only X" (X being whatever word the DM wants to put in the slot).

An example of such a puzzle is "Show Me Only Wealth." Making things tricky, the room may be filled with great wealth, gold, valuables, etc. To solve the puzzle, the players must cover up all the words in the sentence except for "wealth." In this way, they accomplish what the sentence asks, ensuring the only word the stone faces is "wealth."

## 7 Moving Mazes Can Challenge Players
**Mapping Them Can Be a Puzzle for the Dungeon Master Too**

The DM sets up a series of mini-mazes in the Moving Maze puzzle in *Dungeons & Dragons.* The mini mazes can be as big as the DM feels necessary, but 3x3 squares work best for a party to tackle. The mini mazes rotate about in a larger maze, involving 3x3 mini mazes each made of 3x3 squares.

The *D&D* players have a set amount of turns before the maze shifts. There is only one rotation and position that will allow them to progress forward, forcing players to time their movement through the maze and their position accurately to get out.

## 6 Long Distance Statues Can Open Many Doors
**This Puzzle Requires Long-Distance Communication To Solve**

To create a "long distance statues" puzzle, the DM will need two rooms. One room will be full of statues, while the other features a door covered in symbols. The statues correlate to the symbols on the door, and players must align the statues in the statue room to match the design displayed by the door.

When the *D&D* players get the statues in one room to the correct spots, it will unlock the other room's door. This straightforward puzzle can be scaled based on the symbols and statues the DM chooses to use. To raise the difficulty, the DM can substitute letters and symbols for allegories and images.

## 5 A Living Dollhouse Can Turn Ordinary Dungeons Inside Out
**These Miniature Models Hide Dungeon Secrets**

Tightly woven dungeons comprised of multiple floors and rooms connected by sprawling halls and passageways are nothing new to the world of *Dungeons & Dragons*. It's in the name, so players can expect to end up in one before long during most campaigns. Usually, any given dungeon sets the parameters for the player party. That is to say, usually, the party doesn't have access to a living dollhouse.

A living dollhouse is, in essence, a miniature mock-up of the very dungeon the party finds themselves in. The same changes occur in the dungeon where the players reside by opening a door or moving a piece of furniture within the dollhouse. As a pseudo-reflection of the real world, living dollhouses can function similarly to mirror puzzles. At the same time, any DM who wants to throw players for a loop could easily draw inspiration from the likes of *Locke & Key* by having an ordinary spider set foot in the dollhouse, leaving the players with a towering version of the arachnid to face off against during their exploration.

## 4 Venn Diagram Puzzles Bring Party Members Closer Together
**Identifying Similarities Gives the Solution to This Puzzle**

Although most adventuring parties are made up of an eclectic group of characters comprised of different types of builds, if not species, there is always something that ties them together. This is exactly what Venn Diagram puzzles lean into, albeit in a far more specific fashion.

A good example of a Venn Diagram puzzle is a room in which the tile or stonework is broken into different colors or schemes, each of which overlaps at some point. Clues for what these different patterns might represent adorn the walls, each invoking different archetypes or ideals. From there, it is up to the players to arrange themselves in the overlapping spaces, which ultimately represent the shared beliefs or aspirations of said player characters. This can also be done with weapons or items with special significance to different party members. These puzzles can lead to profound character development as secret or unspoken connections come to light.

## 3 Games of Confidence Subvert Player Expectations
**Telling the Questioner What They Want To Hear Is the Way Forward**

Wherever there is a towering Sphinx or a Troll under a bridge, there is often a riddle that follows. Most of the time, it is up to players to be clever and quick-witted enough to figure out the answer that the being asking the question is looking for. In games of confidence, however, players need only answer the question with what they want to hear and with enough confidence to convince whoever is asking of the same.

Suppose a party needs to traverse some treacherous pass or crossing to reach their goal, but a diminutive yet powerful being is standing in their way. Now suppose this being asks a question of whether the party or any individual member can answer a riddle that ties back into some great figure from throughout the campaign. Though the character in question might be the factually correct answer, facts don't matter in games of confidence, meaning the player should almost always answer that they are the figure at the heart of the riddle instead.

## 2 Invisible Pathways Force Players to Get Creative — Or Very, Very Lucky
**Players Can’t Trust Their Eyes in This Classic Trap**

Illusory doors and hidden passageways are a dime a dozen in *Dungeons & Dragons*, and they are easy enough to discover when players are given the time they need to snoop around thoroughly. Invisible pathways, on the other hand, are imperceptible to the naked eye, meaning players will have to get creative when it comes to traversing them.

One of the best instances of invisible passages is an invisible bridge, which could easily be situated over a vast, roaring expanse. If waterfalls are nearby, keen-eyed players might notice that the water doesn't always fall straight down when it splashes, almost as if some unseen structure is catching it as it falls. This might lead to an *Indiana Jones* moment where players are tossing whatever they have at their disposal to trace the path they are supposed to take, assuming there isn't some other natural feature highlighting it for them already.

## 1 Additive/Subtractive Color Puzzles Contain Layers in *Dungeons & Dragons*
**A Puzzle That Will Brighten Up Any Dungeon Crawl**

Colors have always played a major role in different types of symbolism, so much so that they can even be a language unto themselves. It is this concept that additive and subtractive color puzzles make use of, and often to more than memorable effect. Typically, additive and subtractive color puzzles employ the use of different colored crystals, gems, or sheets of glass.

When stacked on top of one another or arranged in a certain fashion, the combinations of these colors create new colors that can reveal clues or answer the puzzle altogether. Maybe this is done through a painting that, when viewed through a certain colored lens, reveals a hidden map or message. This could also be done by way of multiple colored figures or gems that require players to line them up in a way that the light passing through them becomes the message they were searching for all along.
