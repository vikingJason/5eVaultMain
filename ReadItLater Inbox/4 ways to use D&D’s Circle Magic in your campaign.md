# [4 ways to use D&D’s Circle Magic in your campaign](https://www.polygon.com/dnd-dungeons-dragons-circle-magic-spells-best-use/)

[Dungeons & Dragons](https://www.polygon.com/dnd-dungeons-dragons/)’ upcoming [Forgotten Realms books](https://www.polygon.com/dnd-dungeons-dragons/444702/dd-will-split-a-new-forgotten-realms-release-across-two-books/), *Heroes of Faerûn and Adventures in Faerûn*, will offer players an extended look at the lands of Toril, the likes of which we haven’t seen in a long time. Besides (hopefully) extensive lore and locations, the books will also contain plenty of new rules, including Circle Magic spells, of which Wizards of the Coast gave a teaser in [a recent post on D&D Beyond](https://www.dndbeyond.com/posts/2083-circle-magic-and-new-spells-gain-mastery-over-the?srsltid=AfmBOopFEpxdwgEsNwHUB1AUTj7Ni-W2pDruyJpcWNgdm3ihjFe5FddG).

Circle Magic allows players to cast massively powerful spells by coordinating a group of spellcasters, one acting as the “lead” and the others, known as “secondary spellcasters,” providing their magic to support and expand the spell.

In terms of game mechanics, any spell that has a casting time of an action or 1 minute or longer and is cast using a spell slot can be cast as a Circle spell. This lets the caster apply one from a list of enhancing effects, including increasing the spell range, its area, or duration, replacing Material components, and more. However, these come at the cost of every secondary caster having to use the Magic action and maintain Concentration for many rounds.

    ![A group of Red Wizards performs a ritual circle magic spell](ReadItLater%20Inbox/assets/4%20ways%20to%20use%20D&D’s%20Circle%20Magic%20in%20your%20campaign-u2Ge5fJ8b5.jpeg)Image: Wizards of the Coast

This is a cool new option that I imagine will very rarely come into play in D&D games due to its complex setup and high resource investment. I’m skeptical that players would plan their character builds around the chance of casting Circle Magic, even if its effect on high-level spells could be quite spectacular (or dreadful, depending on the spell). More than that, this is instead a rare occurrence of a rule that’s built to enhance the storytelling aspect of the game.

Because of that, Dungeon Masters can make the most out of Circle Magic. This feature is designed to make spells bigger, deadlier, and more spectacular. “The ritual” is a common trope in fantasy storytelling, meaning that these new rules can be a great element for a DM to build an adventure, or even a campaign around. Here are four of my favorite ways to incorporate Circle Magic into a game.

## The Classic: Stop the ritual

If you’ve played D&D as long as I have, there was likely a time when a group of evil spellcasters had to be stopped from summoning an eldritch horror into the world or performing any other form of apocalyptic ritual. It’s an old trope, but one that always works for one simple reason: urgency.

D&D isn’t exactly a fast-paced game, but it can become one with the right setup. If your players have a set number of turns to stop the ritual, then that combat will become a lot more frantic and exciting. You can tell them in advance how many rounds the casting time of the spell requires, or not, depending on how vicious you want to be. Be sure to add rules to account for the death of some of the secondary spellcasters before the ritual is over (for example, the casting time may increase).

DM tip: If you want to be REALLY nasty, then set a clock for players’ turn during combat. Once the time is over, that player has to forfeit their turn, regardless of having used all their available actions or not.

## The Reversal: Perform the ritual

In this cool spin on The Classic, your players have to perform and/or protect a Circle Magic spell. It could be the only way to defeat the BBEG of the campaign, for example. Some enemies should feel beyond the characters’ reach, no matter their level, and this is a good way to make the players punch above their weight. Or they could be out to end the world if the party is fully evil-aligned (I’m sure they exist, somewhere!)

The party’s spellcasters could be performing the Circle Magic spell, either as the lead caster or the supporting ones, while the other characters could set up and coordinate the lines of defense. This is one of my favorite scenarios because it encourages players to think strategically and make good use of their resources and preparation time.

DM tip: Treason! Nothing will beat the look on your players’ faces when, right when they think that they’ve won and the ritual is about to be completed, they are betrayed by one of their allies (perhaps one of the supporting casters), flipping the whole situation upside down.

## The Long Road: Gather the components

Powerful spells require rare and expensive Material components, and Circle Magic is no exception. The two spells revealed in Wizards’ sneak peek, Doomtide and Spellfire Storm, both require some pretty fancy baubles to be cast, my favorite being the three black pearls from Pandemonium necessary for Doomtide. For those who don’t know, Pandemonium is the Outer Plane in D&D cosmology where the souls of Chaotic and Neutral Evil individuals are sent after death. I can easily envision an entire mini-campaign focused on navigating this hellscape and acquiring the black pearls.

    ![Art from the Dungeons &amp; Dragons 5th edition Player’s Handbook. Adventurers opening a chest.](ReadItLater%20Inbox/assets/4%20ways%20to%20use%20D&D’s%20Circle%20Magic%20in%20your%20campaign-2Jho2EGUd5.png)Wizards of the Coast

If the casting of the Circle Magic spell is supposed to be the climax of your campaign, then the search for the components is the perfect lead-up to that. You can use it as a way to make your players gain levels before the big final battle, or to introduce relevant NPCs. For example, the minions of the BBEG could be after the same Material component to stop the ritual in its tracks.

DM tip: The switcheroo. Maybe someone stole the precious Material component and swapped it with a fake. Rogues must be rogueing, after all…This is a good way to not make the ritual the end of your campaign. Perhaps the characters fail to stop the BBEG due to this, and now have to navigate a world where evil won. Speaking of which…

## The Apocalypse: Survive the ritual

If your characters are out to stop a Circle Magic spell, rather than perform it, an interesting twist could be that they’re already too late. When they arrive on the scene, the spell has already been cast, and now they have to focus on surviving. Of course, the more dangerous the spell, the better this will play.

The new Doomtide spell is a good example. It’s a nasty customer, forcing everyone caught in its range to make a saving throw or suffer 5d6 of psychic damage. Moreover, those who fail that roll all subtract 1d6 from their saving throws until the end of the next turn. This can lead to a chain reaction of failures and damage that will leave even the toughest characters at 0 hp, with the risk of automatically failing their death saves if they’re not healed.

    ![Final concept art of the Red Wizard council in Dungeons and Dragons: Honor Among Thieves.](ReadItLater%20Inbox/assets/4%20ways%20to%20use%20D&D’s%20Circle%20Magic%20in%20your%20campaign-MY7Tk7WggJ.jpeg)Image: Eleni Roussos/Hasbro/Paramount Pictures/Penguin Random House

Just finding a way to survive that onslaught will make for one memorable game sequence. The players will have to use all their resources smartly and efficiently, and hope that luck is on their side. Even if they survive, they will emerge in an apocalyptic landscape where they will have to deal with the aftermath of their failure to stop the ritual.

DM tip: Unless you’re out for a party wipe, try to set up failsafes to ensure the survival of at least some of the characters. Deus ex machina should be used sparingly, but it can be effective at the right time. Perhaps the characters are saved by the same people they were supposed to protect, and who had their lives ruined by their failure. How will this play out?

Overall, I’m pretty excited for any set of rules that provides good inspiration for storytelling and campaign development. The Circle Magic rules make me hopeful that the new Forgotten Realms books will live up to [my very high expectations](https://www.polygon.com/dnd-dungeons-dragons-heroes-faerun-adventures-forgotten-realms-netheril-fall/).
